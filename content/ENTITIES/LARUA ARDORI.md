# Larua Ardori: Demon of Obsession and Grief.
**Aliases:** The Stone Faced God, The Bed of Stone.
**Symbols:** Featureless faces, white sheets, open graves, bottomless pits, nooses, coffins, lakes.
**Worship:** Honor the dead, remember them always, call them back to you, and never let go.
**Dwelling:** The Gray Marches, the realms of the dead, the hearts of survivors and mourners.
**Equivalents:** Agelasti (Virtudom), the Laruae (Tand), Nysius (Orthian).

The legend of the Laruae has haunted man for most of known history, dating back to the fall of the Throne of Ensas. Malevolent spirits shed from the firmament in the wake of great loss, calling out to men in their dreams. They would feed on their grief, hollowing these men from within as they were slowly given physical form. Those that lent supplication to these spirits would be granted reprieve, given glimpses of those they mourned, only to invite the same sickness of spirit to their communities. It was a Marathan theurgist who would go on to reexamine these myths, dissecting the cults behind their propagation. He would posit that rather than existing as clusters of individual evils, the groups may have been acting in service to one single god. A hydra of borrowed faces and names, host to the grief of all those left behind. In this form, the spirit was believed to be a hollow shell left behind by the goddess Ista, not quite living and yet driven to bear new life in her name.

Reimagined by them as Larua Ardori for the horrific obsession with the dead it seemed to inspire in its worshippers, this entity has maintained a zealous following in spite of many attempts to eradicate its worship from the world. The Orthians pose it as a son, or living machination, of the Exarch Nysius. To the scholars of Virtudom, the spirit is known best as Agelasti, a foul power from the homelands of the Saint-Knight Yvaine who was called upon by the most conniving of heathen kings. It has no specific form, usually depicted as an insubstantial figure whose skin clings flimsily to it in the form of whoever it is impersonating.

Promises of catharsis, reconciliation, and even resurrection lure would-be petitioners into its service, lending them rituals which seem to do little more than feed their own grief. Gates dedicated to it often take the shape of mausoleum arches, silver mirrors, or makeshift shrines cobbled together about the site of someone’s death.
